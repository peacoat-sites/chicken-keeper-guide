# How Chicken Keeper Guide Is Written

Chicken Keeper Guide is a practical resource for backyard and homestead flocks: breeds, coops, feed, egg production and flock health. This page explains, plainly, how the articles are made.

## How articles are made

Articles on Chicken Keeper Guide are drafted with AI writing tools. Each draft starts from public reference sources on the topic, including those listed below, and from recent news when a topic is changing.

Before an article is published, a second automated pass reviews it and removes or rewrites anything it cannot stand behind: statistics, prices or dates it is not confident are correct, studies or organizations that may be misattributed, and personal stories presented as if they happened to the writer.

Articles are not reviewed by a veterinarian before publication. Use them as background for a conversation with one, not as a substitute.

The site owner audits published articles in batches, merging pages that overlap, deleting weak ones and correcting errors.

## Who writes here

The bylines on Chicken Keeper Guide (Carol Thompson, Tom Henderson, Janet Wilson, Mike Carter, Sarah Mills) are pen names, one for each area of coverage. They are not real individuals, they hold no professional licenses, and their profile images are illustrations, not photographs of real people.

## Sources we rely on

- [USDA](https://www.usda.gov/): Agriculture and food safety
- [Penn State Extension: Poultry](https://extension.psu.edu/): University poultry guidance
- [USDA APHIS](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/): Avian health and biosecurity
- [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/): Egg and food safety

Where an article depends on a specific figure, rule or study, it links to or names the source so you can check it.

## Limits of this site

Health articles describe common signs and standard care, but they cannot diagnose a sick bird. For illness or injury, contact a veterinarian who treats poultry or your state's poultry extension service. If several birds die suddenly, or you see swollen heads, purple combs and wattles, or a sharp drop in laying with breathing trouble, report it to your state veterinarian or USDA, because these can be signs of avian influenza.

## Corrections

If you find an error, please report it through the [contact page](/contact/). Reports go directly to the site owner, and confirmed errors are corrected in the article.

## How the site makes money

Chicken Keeper Guide may earn money from display advertising and from affiliate links, including Amazon links (as an Amazon Associate, the site earns from qualifying purchases). Advertisers and affiliate partners do not see or approve articles before they are published.

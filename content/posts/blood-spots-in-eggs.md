Blood spots in eggs are safe to eat. That's the short answer, and it's the one most people need before they panic and throw out a dozen eggs. The spot has nothing to do with fertilization, contamination, or a sick hen. It's a small rupture in a blood vessel on the follicle or oviduct wall, and it gets incorporated into the egg before the shell forms.

That said, there's a lot worth understanding here, because the frequency of blood spots in your [backyard flock](/roosters-in-backyard-flock/) can tell you something real about hen health, breed genetics, and feed.

## What Actually Causes Them

A blood spot forms when a tiny capillary ruptures at the moment a yolk is released from the ovary, or less commonly, along the wall of the oviduct. The result is either a spot on the yolk itself (most common) or a streak through the white (less common, often called a meat spot though that's slightly different).

Genetics is a bigger factor than most keepers realize. Brown-egg breeds produce blood spots at a meaningfully higher rate than white-egg breeds. [Rhode Island](/rhode-island-red-chickens/) Reds, Plymouth Rocks, and other dual-purpose brown-egg layers are known to have higher incidence. White Leghorns, by comparison, rarely show them. If your flock is all Barred Rocks and you're seeing spots regularly, that's partly just breed tendency.

Age matters too. As hens get older, the vessels around the follicle become more fragile. A pullet in her first laying year will generally produce cleaner eggs than a three-year-old hen.

Nutritional deficiencies, particularly in vitamin A and vitamin K, make blood vessels more fragile and increase frequency. Hens on a balanced, properly stored [layer feed](/layer-feed-vs-starter-feed/) shouldn't have this problem. But feed that's been sitting in a hot barn for three months is losing vitamin potency, and that shows up in egg quality before it shows up anywhere else. Buy feed in quantities you'll go through in four to six weeks.

## Backyard vs. Commercial Eggs

Commercial producers candle every egg on a conveyor using a bright light that reveals internal defects. Eggs with visible blood spots get pulled and either sold as "processed" (used in liquid or powdered eggs) or discarded. That's why you rarely find one in a store carton.

Backyard flocks don't get candled. You're seeing the full, unfiltered output of your hens, which means you'll encounter blood spots more often than you're used to if you switched from store-bought. This isn't a flock health crisis. It's just the reality of small-scale production.

**Example:** A flock of eight Rhode Island Reds in year two of laying might produce one or two blood-spotted eggs per week. That's not unusual, and it doesn't warrant a vet call or feed change on its own. If you're suddenly seeing spots in four or five eggs daily across a mixed flock, then it's worth looking at feed freshness and vitamin content.

## What to Check (And When to Actually Worry)

A single blood spot now and then? Ignore it or remove it and cook the egg. A sharp uptick across multiple hens in a short window is worth investigating.

Work through these in order:

1. **Check feed age and storage.** Is the bag from more than six weeks ago? Is it stored somewhere that gets hot? Replace it and see if frequency drops over the next two to three weeks.
2. **Look at your supplement situation.** If you're feeding layer pellets from a reputable brand (Purina Layena, Nutrena NatureWise, Kalmbach, and others are common choices), vitamin content should be adequate. If you're mixing your own scratch-heavy diet, you may be diluting nutrients below functional levels.
3. **Consider hen age.** If your oldest hens are the most affected, that's normal aging. It's not preventable.
4. **Rule out stress.** Sudden flock stress (a predator scare, a move, adding new birds) can temporarily spike blood spot frequency. It usually resolves on its own in a week or two.

There's no documented disease that causes *only* blood spots as a symptom. If a hen is also showing signs of illness (lethargy, weight loss, abnormal droppings), that's a separate conversation that warrants a vet visit. Blood spots alone don't point to disease.

## Spot Frequency by Breed Type

| Breed Type | Egg Color | Blood Spot Incidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| White Leghorn | White | Low (under 1%) | Historically selected for clean candling |
| Rhode Island Red | Brown | Moderate to high (up to 6%) | Common in backyard flocks |
| Plymouth Rock | Brown | Moderate | Similar to RIR |
| Australorp | Brown | Moderate | Heavy layers, age-related increase |
| Easter Egger / Ameraucana | Blue/Green | Low to moderate | Variable by individual hen |
| Marans | Dark brown | Moderate | Dark shell makes candling harder |

Incidence figures above are general ranges drawn from poultry science references; individual flock results vary based on management, diet, and genetics.

## Handling Spotted Eggs

You don't need to discard the egg. Remove the spot with the tip of a clean knife or spoon, then cook normally. The egg white, yolk, and shell membrane are not compromised. The FDA and USDA both consider blood-spotted eggs safe to eat, and there's no food safety argument for throwing them away.

One practical note: if you sell eggs from your flock (even informally to neighbors), it's worth candling them first. Most buyers haven't been told what a blood spot is and will assume something is wrong.

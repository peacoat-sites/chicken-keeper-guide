Seventy-two hours. That's roughly how long a [sick chicken](/sick-chicken-symptoms/) has before what starts as "a little off" can turn into something you can't come back from. I've watched that window close faster than I expected, more than once, and I'd rather you didn't learn it the same way I did.

If you're reading this, you've probably got a hen standing in the corner of the run, feathers puffed out like a tennis ball, eyes half-closed, not interested in scratch or anything else you're offering. Maybe she was fine yesterday. Maybe she's been "a little slow" for a couple of days and you've been hoping she'd snap out of it. I'm not going to make you feel bad about that. Every keeper I know has done the watch-and-wait thing, and sometimes it works. But a chicken that's fluffed up and lethargic is always telling you something, and the question is whether you can figure out what before that 72-hour window matters.

Here's what I tell people who reach out with this scenario: don't start by Googling symptoms and spiraling. Start by actually looking at the bird. I mean really looking.

## Start With Your Eyes, Not Google

Pick the bird up. I know that sounds obvious, but a lot of people stand at the edge of the run and observe from five feet away. You need her in your hands. Feel her keel bone, that ridge running down the center of her chest. If you can wrap your fingers around it and it feels sharp, like a knife edge, she's lost weight, possibly a lot of it, and this has been going on longer than you think. A healthy laying hen has some muscle on either side of that keel.

Now check three things in order:

**The vent.** Is there discharge? Dried feces caked to the feathers (called "poopy butt" in most backyard forums, "pasty vent" more formally)? Does the area look swollen, straining, or wet? A vent that's moist and pulsing is a red flag for egg binding. A vent caked with yellow or greenish discharge points toward a possible infection.

**The crop.** First thing in the morning, before she's eaten, her crop should be empty and flat. If it's a hard, golf-ball lump, that's impacted crop. If it's squishy and smells sour, like vinegar or fermented grain, that's sour crop, a yeast overgrowth. Both can make a hen look terrible. Both require different interventions.

**Her breathing.** Watch her chest and listen. Any clicking, wheezing, or rattling when she exhales? Open-mouth breathing (when it's not hot out) is a serious sign. Pull her feathers back and look at her face, swelling around the sinuses or under the eyes, or discharge from the nostrils, means you're probably dealing with a respiratory infection.

## What's Actually Going On: The Most Likely Causes

I've been keeping chickens since 2016, across two different properties, and I've seen a lot of "fluffed and lethargic" presentations. Here's what I've learned about what's actually behind them, ranked roughly by how often I've encountered each.

**Respiratory illness** is the most common culprit in my experience, things like Mycoplasma gallisepticum (MG), infectious bronchitis, or Newcastle disease. These are often introduced by new birds, wild birds, or even on your clothes and boots if you've visited another flock. The Merck Veterinary Manual notes that MG alone is present in an estimated 70-80% of commercial flocks at any given time, and backyard flocks aren't immune. Symptoms you'll see: nasal discharge, swelling around the eyes, rattling breath, and yes, that classic puffed-up posture because a sick chicken conserves heat by fluffing.

**Egg binding** scares me more than respiratory illness because it kills faster. A hen that's straining, walking like a penguin, has a swollen abdomen, and looks completely miserable might have an egg stuck. The longer it sits, it can rupture internally or compress her kidneys. A reader from central Wisconsin emailed me in spring of this year, her 2-year-old Buff Orpington had been "off" for a day and a half before she checked and felt an egg just inside the vent. She did warm soaks (15-20 minutes in a tub of warm, not hot, water), added a few drops of olive oil around the vent, and kept the hen in a warm, dark, quiet space. The egg passed on its own about 6 hours later. That's the best-case scenario. Not everyone gets that outcome.

**Coccidiosis** hits hard in wet weather and especially in younger birds, but adults aren't immune, particularly if they're introduced to a new property with different strains of Eimeria in the soil. I've seen it wipe out a batch of 8-week-old pullets in under a week. The giveaway is often bloody or very dark, mucus-heavy droppings, though not always. Corid (amprolium) is the go-to treatment: 9.6% liquid at 2 teaspoons per gallon of water for 5-7 days, as the bird's only water source. The powder form is available for around $15-20 at Tractor Supply or on Amazon (the site may earn a small commission on purchases through product links).

**Marek's disease** is the one nobody wants to hear. It's a herpesvirus that can cause neurological symptoms, tumors, and immune suppression. A young hen (usually under 2 years old) that suddenly goes limp or off-balance, loses weight rapidly, and can't stand may be showing Marek's signs. There is no treatment. If you vaccinate your chicks at hatch, you dramatically reduce the severity, but the Marek's vaccine doesn't prevent exposure, it prevents tumor formation. This is one of the cases where I don't have a happy ending for you, and I'd rather be straight with you than pretend otherwise.

## A Symptom-to-Cause Reference

The table below reflects what I've seen over a decade and aligns with guidance from the University of California Cooperative Extension's poultry health resources. I don't have exact prevalence percentages for [backyard flocks](/drug-resistant-salmonella-is-surging-in-backyard-flocks-right-now/) specifically, that data is frustratingly hard to find, but these associations are solid.

|---|---|---|---|
| Puffed, lethargic, raspy breathing, nasal discharge | Respiratory infection (MG, IB, Newcastle) | Isolate; supportive care; consider Tylan 50 | Yes, ideally |
| Straining, penguin walk, swollen abdomen | Egg binding | Warm soak 15-20 min; lubricate vent | Yes if no passage in 12h |
| Bloody/dark mucousy droppings, lethargy | Coccidiosis | Corid in water immediately | If not improving in 48h |
| Young bird, leg weakness, paralysis, weight loss | Marek's disease | Supportive care; isolate | Yes |
| Squishy, sour-smelling crop in the morning | Sour crop | Massage, restrict feed, antifungal | If severe/chronic |

## The Isolation Step Most People Skip

The moment you have a sick bird, she goes in a separate space. Not tomorrow, not after you figure out what's wrong. Now. I set up a large dog crate (I use a 42-inch Midwest iCrate) in my garage with a [heat lamp](/heat-lamp-for-chicks/) or a Sweeter Heater panel clipped to the side, keeping ambient temperature around 85-90°F. Sick chickens can't regulate their body temperature well, that's half of why they're puffing their feathers, to trap warmth. Keeping them warm reduces the metabolic load on their system while everything else is wrong.

Put fresh water with electrolytes (I use Sav-A-Chick, about $6 for a pack of three 9.4g packets) and some wet, easy-to-eat feed in there. Scrambled eggs, plain yogurt if she'll eat it, or softened crumble. Don't force feed.

I thought for years that I could assess a sick bird while she was still in the flock. You can't. The other hens will peck her if she's weak, she'll go to the wrong spot to sleep, and you'll lose track of whether she's eating or drinking. Isolation isn't optional.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1:** A 3-year-old Rhode Island Red, puffed and not eating for 24 hours, breathing audibly rattly. Owner isolated her, started Tylan 50 injections (0.5cc subcutaneously once daily for 5 days, ask a vet to show you the first one) and supportive electrolyte water. Symptoms improved by day 3, back with the flock on day 8. Full recovery. This is respiratory illness caught early.

**Example 2:** 6-week-old Australorp pullets in an outdoor run after 3 days of rain. Three birds fluffed and lethargic, one with bloody droppings. Owner started Corid at 2 teaspoons per gallon immediately. Two birds recovered within 72 hours. One was too far gone and died on day 2. The lesson: with coccidiosis, every hour counts, especially in chicks.

**Example 3:** Hen that had been laying irregularly, suddenly lethargic for 36 hours, straining when observed. Warm soaks twice a day for two days. No egg passed. Vet visit on day 2 confirmed internal laying and early peritonitis. The bird was humanely euthanized. This is why "wait and see" past 24 hours on a suspected egg binding is a real risk.

  - q: "Can I make my own scratch mix at home?"
    a: "Yes, and it's actually pretty easy. Buy whole or cracked grains in bulk from a feed supplier or online (wheat berries, oats, cracked corn, sunflower seeds) and mix to taste. The advantage is you control what's in it and can skip filler grains like milo if you want. You won't save huge money unless you're buying in 100+ pound quantities, but the nutrition quality tends to be better than bargain commercial mixes."
  - q: "Does scratch replace oyster shell or grit?"
    a: "No, not even close. Scratch is food. Oyster shell provides calcium for eggshell formation and needs to be offered separately, free choice. Insoluble granite grit helps hens grind whole grains in their gizzard and is especially important if you're feeding whole corn or large seeds rather than cracked grains. Don't let one substitute for the other."
  - q: "Is scratch safe for chicks?"
    a: "Not really, especially not in the first 8 weeks. Chicks need high-protein chick starter (18-20% protein) for development, and even small amounts of scratch can displace that nutrition. If you do offer scratch to chicks older than 6-8 weeks, they need grit available first or the whole grains can cause crop and digestive problems."
  - q: "Why are my chickens ignoring their layer feed and just waiting for scratch?"
    a: "Because you've trained them to. Chickens are smart about energy-dense food, and if they know scratch is coming, they'll hold out for it. The fix is straightforward: stop offering scratch at predictable times, reduce the quantity, and make sure layer pellets are available free-choice all day. The birds will adjust within a week or two once they figure out the scratch windfall isn't coming."
  - q: "Should I stop feeding scratch in summer completely?"
    a: "I wouldn't say completely, but I'd cut it back sharply, especially during heat waves. A small scatter in the cool of early morning is probably fine for most climates. Where I'd draw the hard line is double feedings during days over 90°F, particularly for heavy or feathered-leg breeds that struggle with heat regulation anyway. Light breeds like Leghorns handle heat better and I'd be less worried about a modest scratch ration for them even in summer."

Most [chicken keepers](/egg-prices-crashed-but-chicken-keepers-are-staying-put/) buy their first bag of scratch without really knowing what it is. I did. I grabbed a 50-pound bag of Purina Scratch Grains off the feed store shelf about nine years ago because it was cheap, the chickens went absolutely crazy for it, and honestly I assumed "scratch" was just another word for chicken food. It took me embarrassingly long to figure out I'd been partially undermining my own flock's nutrition for months.

Here's the thing nobody says clearly enough upfront: scratch grains are a treat. Full stop. They're not a feed replacement, not a nutritional supplement, not a substitute for layer pellets. They're the chicken equivalent of popcorn. And just like popcorn, a little is fine and a lot is a problem.

That framing changed how I use scratch entirely, and I think it'll change how you use it too.

## What Scratch Actually Is (And What's in the Bag)

"Scratch grains" refers to any mix of cracked, rolled, or whole grains fed as a supplement to poultry. The name comes from the old practice of scattering grains on the ground so chickens would scratch around to find them, which gives them exercise and mimics foraging behavior. That part is genuinely good. The behavioral enrichment is real.

What's inside the bag varies a lot by brand and price point. Cheap scratch is often just cracked corn and maybe some milo (grain sorghum). Mid-range products like Purina Scratch Grains or Kalmbach Feeds Scratch typically add wheat, milo, and oats to the corn base. Premium blends from producers like Scratch and Peck Feeds layer in sunflower seeds, peas, and sometimes barley. I've seen bags labeled "scratch" that were 90% cracked corn and bags that had eight or nine different grains. The label tells you what's inside, but a lot of keepers don't bother reading it.

What surprised me was how dramatically the protein content swings between products. When I finally started comparing nutrition tags side by side, some of the bargain scratch bags listed protein at 7%, while layer pellets from the same shelf ran 16-18%. That's not a small difference. If a hen is filling up on scratch, she's leaving less room in her gut for the feed that actually supports [egg production](/egg-production-by-breed-2026/) and feather quality.

## The Heat Metabolism Factor

This is the part of scratch science I find genuinely interesting, and it's also where the conventional advice gets nuanced.

Digesting high-carbohydrate grains generates body heat through a process called thermogenesis. Scratch grains, being mostly carbs, produce more metabolic warmth during digestion than protein or fat sources. This is useful in winter: scattering scratch in the coop an hour or two before the hens go to roost gives them a slow internal heat source through the night. I've been doing this for years, especially when overnight temps drop below 20°F. The research supporting this is reasonably solid, though the practical magnitude varies by bird size and coop insulation.

The flip side is summer. Feeding large amounts of scratch when it's 95°F outside adds internal heat load to birds that are already struggling. This genuinely can contribute to heat stress in heavy breeds like Buff Orpingtons or Jersey Giants. I made this mistake during a brutal July [heat wave](/how-to-keep-chickens-cool-during-a-heat-wave/) years ago, kept up my usual generous scratch habit, and noticed my Barred Rocks panting more than I'd expect. Once I cut scratch back to almost nothing and focused on cold watermelon, electrolyte water, and shade, they settled down. Correlation isn't causation, but I haven't pushed scratch hard in summer since.

## How Much Is Actually Too Much

The standard recommendation is that scratch should make up no more than 10% of a hen's daily intake. In practice, that's roughly 1-2 tablespoons per bird per day, not a generous double handful scattered around the whole run every morning and afternoon.

I'll be honest: I've tested what happens when you push past that threshold over time. A few years ago I had a reader (Sarah from central Ohio, emailed me in late fall) who was convinced her flock of 14 Rhode Island Reds had just "stopped laying for the season." After some back and forth about their diet, it came out that she'd been feeding scratch twice a day, probably 1/3 cup per bird per feeding, because she liked watching them run for it. That's a lot of empty carbohydrate calories displacing layer feed protein.

Scenario: 14 Rhode Island Reds, heavy scratch feeding (~30% of diet by volume) over 6 weeks in fall. Sarah reduced scratch to a single small scatter in the morning (about 1 tablespoon per bird), ensured free-choice layer pellets all day, and added oyster shell on the side. Result: egg production went from 3-4 eggs daily back up to 9-11 within 3 weeks. Not a dramatic turnaround time, but steady and clear.

The research here is genuinely mixed on exact thresholds, and I don't want to pretend there's a precise number that applies to every flock size, breed, or climate. But the 10% guideline is a reasonable starting point, and the consequences of exceeding it show up in egg production data fairly quickly.

## What to Do With It (Practically)

The best uses I've found for scratch:

Scatter it in the run over fresh straw or leaves. The foraging behavior this creates keeps bored birds occupied for a surprisingly long time. It's cheap entertainment for birds that would otherwise be picking at each other.

Use it as a training reward. Chickens learn to come when called faster than most people expect, and scratch is a better "recall treat" than most options because it's dry, easy to scatter, and doesn't attract flies the way fruit or table scraps do.

Winter evening feeding, about an hour before roost. Small amount, whole corn or a corn-heavy mix, timed to give them something to digest overnight. In my experience with harsh midwest winters, birds that get a small evening scratch ration seem to roost tighter and more calmly than those that don't. I can't prove causation, but I've kept doing it.

What I wouldn't do: free-choice scratch in a feeder. I see this sometimes and it's a real nutritional mistake. When scratch is available 24/7, hens self-select for the carbohydrates over their layer pellets, probably because it tastes better to them, and protein intake tanks.

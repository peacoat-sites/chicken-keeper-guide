Double yolk eggs are safe to eat, completely normal to find, and not a sign that something is wrong with your flock. That said, they do tell you something specific about your hens, and if you're suddenly getting a lot of them, it's worth understanding why.

A double yolk forms when a hen releases two yolks into the oviduct too close together. Both get wrapped in the same albumen and shell. It's a timing glitch, not a defect. Young pullets are most likely to produce double yolkers because their laying cycle hasn't fully regulated yet. High-production breeds also throw them more frequently than average, and some individual hens seem genetically predisposed to producing them throughout their entire laying lives.

## Why Some Hens Produce Them More Than Others

Genetics matter here. High-production hybrid layers like Golden Comets, Black Sex-Links, and production Reds are engineered to lay fast and frequently, which increases the chance of a yolk timing error. Heritage breeds like Dominiques or Buckeyes produce them too, just less often.

Body size plays a role as well. Larger hens, specifically breeds that are heavy-bodied but also high-production like Buff Orpingtons, seem to have a higher incidence than lighter Mediterranean breeds. The research on this is not extensive, but experienced breeders and hatcheries have noted the pattern for decades.

Pullets between about 20 and 30 weeks old are your most likely source. Their hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (the hormone loop that controls ovulation timing) is still calibrating. During that window, double yolkers might show up two or three times a week from the same bird. It usually evens out once she's been laying consistently for a couple months.

## The Nutritional Picture

| Component | Standard Large Egg (single yolk) | Double Yolk Egg (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| Calories | ~72 kcal | ~120-130 kcal |
| Protein | ~6.3 g | ~8-9 g |
| Total Fat | ~5 g | ~8-10 g |
| Cholesterol | ~186 mg | ~370 mg |
| Yolk weight | ~17 g | ~32-34 g |

The white is roughly the same as a standard egg. The difference is almost entirely in the yolk volume. That means more fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), more choline, more cholesterol. If you're baking, a double yolker will add richness you weren't accounting for in a recipe that calls for a single egg.

## Does a Double Yolk Mean Your Hen Is Stressed?

In pullets, no. It's just normal developmental irregularity. In a fully mature hen who hasn't produced them before, a cluster of double yolkers can indicate that something disrupted her laying cycle and she's restarting it. Molt is the most common trigger. A short day length in November or December can do it. A predator scare, a move to a new coop, or a change in feed can all interrupt the cycle enough to cause a few abnormal ovulations when it restarts.

If you're seeing multiple double yolkers per week from hens who are more than six months into laying, check for stressors before assuming it's a problem. It usually isn't, but it's your flock's way of flagging that something shifted.

## Incubating Double Yolk Eggs

Don't. Or at least, don't expect results. The two embryos almost always interfere with each other during development. They share the same air cell and limited space inside the shell. Even in controlled hatchery conditions with candling and manual intervention at lockdown, the survival rate is extremely low. You can find videos of people successfully hatching double yolk eggs with careful assisted hatching, but those are outliers, not a repeatable method for a backyard setting.

If you want to incubate, set that egg aside and eat it. Use normally shaped, standard-sized eggs from healthy mature hens.

## Spotting Them Before You Crack Them

You can often identify a double yolker before it's opened. They're noticeably larger than the rest of the clutch, often elongated or slightly misshapen at the larger end. Candling confirms it quickly: hold a bright light (a dedicated [egg candler like this one](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=egg+candler+poultry) works far better than a flashlight) against the blunt end in a dark room. Two distinct yolk shadows will be visible, often shifting around each other when you tilt the egg.

Commercial egg producers actually candle for double yolkers and sort them specifically. Some retailers sell them as a novelty at a premium price. That should tell you something about how common they actually are in high-production flocks.

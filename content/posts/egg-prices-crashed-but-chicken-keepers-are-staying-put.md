If you started keeping chickens because eggs hit $6.23 a dozen last March, you've probably noticed something by now: the price came back down. Way down. The USDA's Bureau of Labor Statistics reported the average retail price for a dozen eggs had fallen to $2.19 by May 2026, which is, depending on your flock size and feed bill, roughly what your hens are costing you per dozen right now. So you might be wondering whether this whole [backyard chicken](/backyard-chicken-egg-cost-vs-store/) thing still makes sense, whether you're the only one sticking with it, or whether you made a mistake buying that coop last spring. Here's what I tell people who ask me that: look around. Nobody's selling their birds.

The numbers back that up. The American Pet Products Association counted roughly 11 million U.S. households keeping chickens in 2025, nearly double the 5.8 million recorded in 2018. Google Trends data shows searches for "[backyard chickens](/are-you-ready-to-raise-backyard-chickens/)" and "how to build a chicken coop" surging again in 2026, and this time it's not being driven by panic at the grocery store. Something else is happening. Tractor Supply CEO Hal Lawton put it plainly: "nearly 1 in 5 of our customers raise chickens now," and he confirmed the company saw no slowdown in the category even as egg prices fell. That's a retailer watching purchasing behavior in real time, and what they're seeing is a category that has graduated from trend to lifestyle.

## The Economics Changed, But the People Didn't Leave

This is the part that surprises a lot of folks. The original pitch for backyard chickens, at least the one that went viral in 2025, was straightforward: eggs are expensive, feed is cheap, do the math. Except the math never quite worked, even at $6 a dozen. A laying hen eats roughly 1.5 to 2 pounds of quality feed per week. With layer pellets running $25 to $35 for a 50-pound bag in most parts of the country [right now](/drug-resistant-salmonella-is-spreading-from-backyard-flocks-right-now/), and the average hen producing maybe 250 to 280 eggs per year in her prime, your cost per dozen is almost always higher than the store shelf price. It was true when eggs were $6. It's definitely true at $2.19.

Here's a quick look at how the math actually shakes out for a small backyard flock right now:

| Cost factor | Estimate (4-hen flock, per year) |
|---|---|
| Layer feed (4 hens × ~90 lbs each) | $135 – $180 |
| Bedding (pine shavings, straw) | $60 – $100 |
| Coop setup (amortized over 10 years) | $50 – $150 |
| Miscellaneous (grit, oyster shell, vet) | $40 – $80 |
| **Total annual cost** | **$285 – $510** |
| Eggs produced (4 hens × ~250 eggs) | ~1,000 eggs (~83 dozen) |
| **Your cost per dozen** | **$3.43 – $6.14** |

At $2.19 a dozen in the store, you are not coming out ahead. And people know this. The Yahoo/Campus Insights piece from July 2026 said it plainly in the headline: raising backyard chickens won't guarantee you a profit. The story is that people are doing it anyway, and not because they're bad at math.

## What the "Pet" Shift Actually Means for How You Keep Chickens

A 2024 University of Winchester survey of more than 2,000 chicken owners found that the language people used to describe their birds was "very suggestive" of viewing them as pets, not livestock. Names. Personalities. Specific preferences for certain hens. Grief when they lose one. If you've had chickens for more than a season, none of this surprises you, because it happened to you too. You didn't plan on having a favorite. Then Marge started following you around the yard, and here we are.

This shift has real implications for how you should be thinking about your flock and your setup. If your hens are companions first, the calculus on spending changes. A $400 predator-proof coop makes sense. So does a vet visit for a bird with sour crop, which a purely production-minded keeper might not bother with. Breeds matter more, too, when personality is part of the equation. Buff Orpingtons, Australorps, and Speckled Sussex consistently rank as the friendliest and most handleable breeds for backyard situations. Leghorns will out-produce all of them, but they're flighty and not especially interested in you.

## The Cultural Moment That's Keeping Google Searches Climbing

In July 2026, World Magazine reported that Vice President JD Vance's family added chickens to the vice-presidential residence, and that their elaborate coop setup had "inspired renewed interest in tending backyard chickens." That's a very specific kind of signal. When keeping chickens becomes something a sitting VP talks about publicly, it's no longer a fringe homesteader hobby or a pandemic-era coping mechanism. It's normalized in a way that's hard to walk back.

The search trends mirror this. The 2025 spike in "backyard chickens" searches was clearly price-driven. The 2026 wave looks different in character, pulling in people who aren't running from grocery bills but toward something, a slower pace, more connection to their food, an animal that greets them in the morning. That's not a demographic you lose when egg prices normalize.

## What New Keepers in 2026 Are Actually Getting Into

If you're just starting out, or you bought chicks this spring and they're about to start laying, here's what I wish someone had said to me before my first flock. The feed cost is real and ongoing. The predator pressure is real and relentless. Hawks, raccoons, opossums, neighborhood dogs: if there's a gap in your coop, something will find it. Hardware cloth, not chicken wire, on every opening. A solid latch, not a simple hook, on every door.

The AOL beginner's guide from July 2026 lays out the basic coop requirements well: 4 square feet of indoor space per bird, 10 square feet of outdoor run space minimum, proper ventilation at the roofline, and nest boxes at roughly one per three to four hens. Those aren't suggestions. A crowded, poorly ventilated coop creates respiratory illness, feather pecking, and misery for everyone involved.

The good news is that the community of people doing this well has never been bigger. Eleven million households means there's almost certainly a local Facebook group, a feed store with knowledgeable staff, or a neighbor two streets over who can tell you why your hen stopped laying in August. You're not doing this alone.

Egg prices will do what they do. They went up, they came down, they'll move again. But the people who stayed, and the people just arriving now, aren't tracking the commodity markets anymore. They're watching their birds figure out a new treat, or standing in the yard at dusk counting heads before closing the coop door. That habit, once it's yours, turns out to be remarkably hard to give up.

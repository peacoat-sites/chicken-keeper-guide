Wild birds are moving. Right now, in November 2026, tens of millions of ducks, geese, and shorebirds are funneling down the Atlantic, Mississippi, Central, and Pacific flyways across North America, and the same thing is happening across the British Isles and continental Europe. Every one of those flyways passes directly over neighborhoods where people keep [backyard chickens](/what-the-vance-coop-tells-us-about-keeping-backyard-chickens/), ducks, and turkeys. And this year, health agencies on both sides of the Atlantic are saying out loud what most small flock owners haven't fully absorbed: this is the most dangerous window of the year, and it's open right now.

The numbers from just the past five weeks make the point plainly. California's CDFA reported 7 HPAI detections between October 5 and November 4, 2026. Six of those were in [backyard flocks](/new-usda-egg-label-rules-give-backyard-flocks-a-competitive-edge/), spread across Sacramento, Solano, Stanislaus, and San Benito counties. Iowa confirmed HPAI in a 138-bird Boone County backyard flock, the state's 7th detection of 2026 and its first since March, timed almost exactly to migration season ramping back up. On November 17, 2026, Britain's DEFRA issued a mandatory nationwide housing order, requiring every flock in England, commercial or backyard, to be kept indoors as Great Britain logged 20 HPAI-positive wild birds between October 1 and November 6. These aren't scattered data points. They're a pattern with a calendar attached to it.

The CDC recorded 72 total human H5 cases in the U.S. since February 2024, with 2 of those cases linked specifically to backyard flock exposure, according to reporting on November 3, 2026. That number isn't catastrophic by pandemic standards, but it signals something important: [backyard keepers](/new-usda-poultry-rules-are-now-in-effect-here-is-what-backyard-keepers/) are no longer bystanders in this outbreak.

## Why Fall Migration Creates a Specific, Predictable Threat

HPAI H5N1 spreads through the feces, respiratory secretions, and feathers of infected birds. Wild waterfowl, especially dabbling ducks like mallards, can carry the virus without showing obvious signs of illness. When a flock of migrating geese or ducks rests on a pond, in a field, or on a rooftop near your coop, they leave virus particles behind in their droppings. Your chickens don't need direct contact. Contaminated mud tracked in on boots, wild bird feces landing in an open run, or a shared water source is enough.

Avian influenza cases in wild birds follow a documented seasonal pattern that peaks in late fall and winter. November through February is the most dangerous stretch, full stop. That's not a vague warning, it's the specific window when wild bird density over populated areas is highest and the virus moves fastest between wild and domestic populations.

## What Agencies Are Actually Telling You to Do

Wisconsin's DATCP, alongside USDA and CDC, issued a joint advisory on November 8, 2026, urging backyard flock owners to keep birds indoors during migration and to quarantine any new birds for at least 30 days. That 30-day quarantine recommendation is longer than what many keepers follow. A lot of people do 7 to 14 days. Right now, 30 days is the floor.

Britain went further. DEFRA's November 17 mandatory housing order doesn't distinguish between a 10,000-bird commercial operation and someone with six hens in a garden. All birds, indoors, no exceptions. England has used these housing orders before during past HPAI surges, and they work, because the single biggest transmission vector is wild-to-domestic contact.

The U.S. hasn't issued a federal housing mandate, but state-level pressure is real. If you're in California, Iowa, or Wisconsin right now, your state agency is already telling you that free-range access during migration is a calculated risk, not just an abstract one.

## The Practical Biosecurity Steps That Actually Matter

There's a gap between "good biosecurity" as a concept and what it means on a Tuesday morning when you're doing chores. Here's where the real exposure points are:

**Feed and water.** Open feeders attract wild birds. If your feeders are outside and accessible, you're inviting sparrows, starlings, and migrating waterfowl to eat alongside your flock. Move feed inside the coop or under a covered structure with wire sides that prevent wild bird access. Same goes for water.

**Your own feet and clothes.** Fomite transmission is real. Dedicated coop footwear and a boot wash with a virucidal disinfectant (a dilute Virkon S solution, for example) at the coop entrance isn't overkill right now.

**New birds.** Don't bring new birds onto your property in November unless you're committed to a true 30-day separate-space quarantine, in a separate building, with separate tools, before any contact with your existing flock.

**Dead wild birds nearby.** Don't handle them with bare hands. Report them to your state ag department or wildlife agency. A dead duck in your yard or on a nearby pond right now deserves a phone call.

## Housing Your Flock: Temporary Confinement Without Destroying Your Coop Setup

The objection most backyard keepers have to housing orders or indoor confinement is real: their coops weren't designed for 24-hour confinement. A flock that free-ranges all day needs more square footage, more enrichment, and more ventilation management when confined. The general rule is 4 square feet of indoor space per standard-sized hen. If you're under that, you're going to see stress behaviors like feather pecking within days.

Here's a comparison of temporary confinement approaches for backyard flocks during a migration-risk window:

| Approach | Cost | Predator/Wild Bird Protection | Stress Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full indoor coop lockdown (existing coop) | $0 added cost | High, if coop is solid | Moderate to high if overcrowded |
| Hardware cloth "run tent" or covered run addition | $50–$200 in materials | High, if fully enclosed with 1/2" hardware cloth | Low, birds retain outdoor space |
| Temporary cattle panel hoop run with bird netting | $80–$150 | Moderate (netting, not wire) | Low |
| Existing open run with overhead netting only | Low cost | Low, feces can still fall through | Low |

The hardware cloth enclosed run is the right answer for most backyard setups during this window. Bird netting alone doesn't stop contaminated droppings from wild birds passing through. It has to be a solid barrier.

## The Human Health Piece Shouldn't Be Dismissed

Two of the 72 U.S. human H5 cases recorded since February 2024 involved backyard flock exposure. That's a small number, but the pattern of exposure is worth understanding. In both cases, people were handling sick or dead birds without respiratory protection. The CDC's current guidance for anyone who suspects HPAI in their flock includes wearing an N95 respirator, eye protection, and gloves when handling birds or cleaning the coop.

If your birds show sudden high mortality, neurological symptoms like twisting necks or loss of coordination, swollen faces, or a sharp drop in egg production, call your state veterinarian's office immediately. Don't wait for it to get worse. Early reporting protects your neighbors' flocks too.

November and December are not the months to be casual about any of this. The migration isn't slowing down. The detections keep coming. Keep your birds covered, your feeders inside, and your boots clean.

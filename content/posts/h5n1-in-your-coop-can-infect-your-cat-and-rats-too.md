Peak migration season is here, and backyard flocks across the country are at their highest H5N1 risk of the year. Wisconsin confirmed a new backyard flock infection in Rusk County on November 13, 2026, the same day Delaware's Department of Agriculture issued a migratory-season bird flu warning to [backyard keepers](/new-usda-poultry-rules-are-now-in-effect-here-is-what-backyard-keepers/). California racked up six confirmed backyard flock detections between October 5 and November 4, 2026, spanning Sacramento, Solano, Stanislaus, and San Benito counties, compared to just one commercial flock in the same window. The virus is moving, and it's moving through backyards specifically.

What makes this fall different isn't just the case count. A peer-reviewed study published in *Emerging Infectious Diseases* (Vol. 32, No. 10, 2026) documented something researchers hadn't confirmed before in a backyard setting: H5N1 [spreading from](/drug-resistant-salmonella-is-spreading-from-backyard-flocks-right-now/) a flock to a farm cat and wild rats on the same property. That's not a theoretical risk anymore. It happened on a southern Illinois farm, and the genetic fingerprints of the virus in the cat and rats matched the virus that killed 100 of the farm's 105 birds. If you have chickens, there's a reasonable chance you also have rats and a cat. That combination just got a lot more complicated.

The research came out of the University of Illinois, with Dr. Leyi Wang of the U of I Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory as lead researcher. All three viruses recovered from the Illinois farm, in the birds, the cat, and the rat, carried a mammalian-adaptation marker. That means the virus wasn't just incidentally present in the mammals. It was genetically positioned to spread across species lines.

## The Rat Problem Nobody Wants to Talk About

Backyard keepers spend a lot of energy worrying about wild birds dropping disease into their runs. That concern is legitimate. But the Illinois findings shift the picture. Viral RNA was detected in the infected rat's urine. Rats urinate constantly as they move, which means a single infected rat moving through your feed storage, across your waterers, or under your coop floor isn't just a nuisance. It's a potential contamination event at every step.

Dr. Wang put it plainly: "Since rats live on nearly every farm despite biosecurity, they may be an overlooked link between wild birds, poultry and other mammals." That's not a minor footnote. Rats can pick up H5N1 from contact with infected bird feces or carcasses, and then carry it into spaces where your birds, your cats, and potentially you interact with surfaces daily. Standard biosecurity protocols simply weren't designed with rat-to-poultry urine transmission in mind.

Practical response here isn't complicated, but it requires consistency. Eliminate standing water and spilled feed outside coop hours. Use metal feeders and metal bins for feed storage, not plastic or wood that rats gnaw through in a week. Set snap traps or use a bait station system inside a locked, tamper-resistant box positioned along the walls where rats run. Check and reset weekly. If you're seeing fresh droppings near your waterers or feeders, treat that as an active biosecurity breach, not just a nuisance pest problem.

## What the Cat Infection Actually Means for Your Setup

The farm cat in the Illinois case tested positive in all 12 tissue samples collected. That's a thorough infection, not marginal exposure. Farm cats that hunt rodents or scavenge bird carcasses are exactly the kind of animals that sit at the intersection of wild bird activity, rodent populations, and human households. They move between the coop, the yard, and inside the home freely.

This creates a chain worth thinking through. Wild bird infects flock. Flock infects cat through direct contact or consumption of infected bird material. Cat comes inside. If you're not washing your hands after handling a cat that's been outside during an active outbreak period, you're introducing a new link in that chain. The CDC hasn't documented human infection through cats in the U.S. from this specific pathway, but cats have infected people with H5N1 in documented overseas cases, and the Illinois virus carried mammalian-adaptation markers.

For the November migration window, consider keeping barn cats out of the coop entirely, not just discouraged from it. A determined cat can get into most backyard coops. Latching pop doors and sealing gaps isn't just [about keeping](/what-the-vance-coop-tells-us-about-keeping-backyard-chickens/) predators out. It's now also about keeping your own animals from becoming a secondary exposure route.

## Where the Current Outbreak Stands Heading Into Winter

The California and Wisconsin cases aren't anomalies. They're part of a pattern that follows waterfowl migration routes with uncomfortable accuracy. Here's a snapshot of recent confirmed backyard activity:

|---|---|---|---|
| California | 6 | Oct 5 – Nov 4, 2026 | Sacramento, Solano, Stanislaus, San Benito |
| Wisconsin | 1 (confirmed) | Nov 13, 2026 | Rusk |
| Illinois | 1 (index case in study) | 2025–2026 | Southern Illinois (unnamed) |

Backyard flocks are showing up in these case counts at a higher rate than commercial operations in some states, partly because commercial houses use all-in/all-out biosecurity with covered housing and biosecure footwear protocols that most backyard setups can't replicate. That's not a criticism. It's just the reality of the difference in infrastructure.

## Biosecurity Steps That Actually Address This Research

Standard biosecurity advice, covering runs, washing hands, limiting visitors, is still correct. But the Illinois findings specifically add two things to that list that weren't typically emphasized before.

First, rodent control is now a bird flu biosecurity measure, not a separate pest issue. Treat it as such. Document where you're seeing activity, what bait or traps you're using, and whether pressure is increasing. Second, dead bird protocols matter more than many keepers realize. If you find a dead wild bird on or near your property during migration season, don't handle it without gloves, and don't let your cats or dogs near it. Contact your state veterinarian's office or USDA APHIS Wildlife Services for guidance on reporting and disposal. The Illinois outbreak almost certainly began with wild bird contact. What happened after is what the study documented.

The current H5N1 situation isn't a reason to give up backyard chickens. It is a reason to take the rat runs under your coop seriously, rethink how your cat accesses the bird areas, and pay attention to your state ag department's alerts during November and December when duck and goose migration peaks across most of the country.

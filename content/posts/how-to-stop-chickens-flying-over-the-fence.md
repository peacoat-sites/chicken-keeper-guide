Clipping one wing takes about 90 seconds and costs nothing. That single fact fixes maybe 70% of chicken-escapes, and most people either don't know it or are squeamish about trying it. Both are fixable problems.

The other 30% requires something structural. Some breeds are just wired to fly, some runs are too small and the birds are motivated to leave, and some people have already clipped wings and watched their Hamburgs clear a six-foot fence anyway. If that's you, keep reading.

## Wing Clipping: The Right Way

Most guides show you a diagram of a chicken wing and tell you to cut the primary flight feathers on one side. That's correct, but the diagram always looks scarier than the job actually is.

Grab the bird, tuck it under your non-dominant arm with its head toward your back (like a football). It'll calm down in about ten seconds. Open the wing and you'll see ten long primary feathers at the tip. Cut them back to about two inches from the covert feathers above them, using sharp scissors or poultry shears. The feathers have no nerve endings where you're cutting; the bird won't flinch. If you see pink, you've gone too deep into a blood feather and need to stop and back up. That's the one thing worth being careful about.

Only clip one side. Clipping both lets the bird maintain symmetry and still get lift. One clipped wing creates torque mid-flight, and most birds stop trying after one awkward, embarrassing landing.

My Buff Orpingtons stayed put for three years on one clip per molt. My Golden-Laced Wyandottes needed it redone after an early molt one year, which I didn't notice for about three weeks until I found two of them in my neighbor's garden. Lesson: check your birds every fall after molt.

## When Clipping Isn't Enough

Here's where I'll be honest about something I got wrong for a long time. I assumed wing clipping was the answer for every bird, and I kept attributing escapes to "not cutting enough." Eventually I had to admit the problem wasn't the clipping technique. It was the breed.

Hamburgs, White Leghorns, Anconas, Campines, and most bantams have the muscle mass and wingspan-to-body-weight ratio to fly functionally even with compromised feathers. A clipped Hamburg isn't a grounded Hamburg, it's a Hamburg that has to work 20% harder to clear your fence. They'll still do it.

For these birds, you have two structural options: a fully enclosed run, or a fence design that defeats flight without requiring a roof.

The angled overhang is underrated. Take your existing fence posts and add a 12-to-18-inch arm angled inward at about 45 degrees. Run wire or netting from those arms back to the fence. A bird that attempts to land on the top of the fence instead lands on an inward-sloping surface it can't grip. I built this on a 5-foot run for my bantam flock using 1-inch conduit bent at a hardware store and basic hex netting. Cost me about $47 in materials and a Saturday morning. Zero escapes since, and that was three years ago.

The full overhead net is the other path. It's more work and more money, but it's also [predator protection](/chicken-coop-predator-protection/), which is a separate argument in its own favor.

## Fence Options: What Actually Works

People overbuild or underbuild, rarely get it right the first time. Here's a practical comparison of what's available.

| Solution | Effective for | Approx. cost (2026) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wing clip (one wing) | Most heavy/dual-purpose breeds | $0 | Must redo after molt |
| 6-ft. wood or vinyl privacy fence | Most breeds except Mediterranean/bantams | $18-$35 per linear foot installed | Also deters some predators |
| 6-ft. chain link + 45° overhang arm | All breeds including strong fliers | $12-$22 per linear foot + ~$3/ft for overhang | DIY-able |
| Full overhead poultry netting | All breeds, no exceptions | $0.15-$0.40 per sq ft for netting; posts extra | Also keeps hawks out |
| Electric poultry fence (low-profile) | Containment of free-range areas | $180-$260 for 164-ft kit (Premier 1) | Better for daytime ranging than permanent runs |

The Premier 1 PoultryNet is what I use for my seasonal free-range rotation, not as a primary run fence. It's excellent at keeping chickens off the garden beds and deterring foxes but a motivated rooster can figure it out in a week.

For permanent run fencing, the angled-arm modification on a 5-foot chain link fence is the best cost-to-performance ratio I've found for problem fliers. Install guide if you're building from scratch:

1. Set 4x4 posts at 8-foot spacing, 2 feet in the ground, concrete-set.
2. Attach chain link or 2x4 welded wire to the posts at your desired height (5-6 feet).
3. Cut 18-inch sections of 3/4-inch conduit and attach them to the post tops with conduit clamps, angled inward at roughly 45 degrees.
4. Run 1-inch hex netting or bird netting from the tips of the conduit arms back to the top of the fence wire, stapling it taut.
5. The whole assembly should lean into the run from the chicken's perspective.

A reader named Marcus in rural Tennessee emailed me last spring after his Leghorn flock was raiding his raised beds daily. He'd clipped wings twice with no success. He built this exact overhang configuration on a 100-foot run perimeter over a weekend. Total material cost: $211. Escapes dropped to zero.

## Motivation Matters

One thing the fence guides don't mention: a chicken that desperately wants to leave tells you something. Overcrowding, boredom, or insufficient forage inside the run are major drivers of escape behavior, sometimes more than breed instinct.

The standard recommendation is 10 square feet per bird in the run. I'd argue 15-20 is where behavior problems start to disappear. If you've got eight birds in a 60-square-foot run, no fence height will fully solve the problem; you'll be treating symptoms. Give them more space, hang a cabbage for enrichment, add a dust bath if there isn't one. Some birds that seemed like chronic escape artists settled down completely once the run expanded.

A flock I managed briefly in 2023 had three Black Sexlinks clearing a five-foot fence almost daily. We expanded their run from about 8 square feet per bird to 18 square feet per bird and added [deep litter](/deep-litter-method-chickens/) composting for scratch activity. The fence-jumping stopped within two weeks. Same birds. Same fence height.

Buying chicks from a local feed store is one of the fastest ways to get started with [backyard chickens](/are-you-ready-to-raise-backyard-chickens/), and it has real advantages over ordering through the mail. You skip the shipping stress, you can see the birds before you buy, and you're often home in under an hour. The catch is that feed stores have quirks that hatchery websites won't warn you about, and walking in unprepared is how people end up with six roosters and a mystery breed.

Feed stores typically receive chicks in late February through May, depending on your region. Some stores take special orders through their hatchery supplier (Hoover's and Privett are two of the most common), which means you can sometimes request specific breeds weeks in advance. Most stores can't sex bantams reliably, and straight-run chicks are the norm for specialty breeds, so plan your numbers accordingly.

## What You're Actually Getting (and From Where)

Feed stores don't hatch [their own](/why-do-chickens-eat-their-own-eggs/) chicks. They order from regional or national hatcheries, usually on a weekly or biweekly schedule during the season. The hatchery matters more than people realize, because breed accuracy, Marek's vaccination, and coccidiosis treatment vary by supplier.

Ask the store: "Who's your hatchery, and are these chicks Marek's vaccinated?" Those two questions will tell you a lot. Most commercial hatcheries do vaccinate for Marek's by default, but it's not universal, and some stores don't track it. If you plan to use medicated [chick starter](/chick-starter-feed/) (which contains amprolium to suppress coccidia), know that medicated feed can interfere with coccidiosis vaccination, not Marek's, that's a different thing. If chicks received a coccidiosis vaccine at the hatchery, skip the medicated feed. If they weren't vaccinated for cocci, medicated starter is a reasonable choice for the first eight weeks.

Breed labeling at feed stores is imprecise. "Red Sex-Links" might be Cinnamon Queens or ISA Browns depending on the hatchery's cross that season. "Ameraucana" at a feed store is almost always an Easter Egger. That's not a scandal, Easter Eggers are great layers of blue or green eggs, but if you need true Ameraucanas for showing, a feed store isn't your source.

## The Price Picture

| Chick type | Typical price range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sexed pullets, common breeds | $4 -- $8 each | Leghorns, Rhode Island Reds, sex-links |
| Sexed pullets, specialty breeds | $7 -- $14 each | Olive Eggers, Lavender Orpingtons, Silkies |
| Straight-run, common breeds | $2 -- $5 each | No sex guarantee; plan for 50% cockerels |
| Bantams (usually straight-run) | $3 -- $6 each | Sexing is rarely offered |
| Special order, minimum qty | Often 3, 6 chicks | Varies by store policy |

Prices have climbed noticeably over the past few years. Expect the higher end of these ranges if you're in a metro area or buying from a farm supply chain rather than an independent store. Some independent stores price below the chains because they're sourcing local or regional hatcheries with lower freight costs.

## Picking Healthy Chicks

This is where you earn back the value of shopping locally rather than ordering online. You can actually look at the birds.

Watch the bin for five minutes before you point at anything. Healthy chicks move around, peck at the brooder floor, drink water, and huddle under heat only briefly. A chick that sits alone with fluffed feathers, eyes half-closed, is sick. A chick that stands with its legs splayed outward or falls sideways when it walks has a leg or neurological problem. Neither is fixable in a home brooder, and both can spread illness to your other birds.

Check vents. Pasty butt, dried fecal matter sealing the vent, is common in shipped chicks under stress, but a bin full of it suggests the store isn't monitoring brooder temperature properly. One or two cases in a bin of thirty isn't alarming. Half the bin showing pasted vents? Walk away.

Respiratory symptoms are a hard no. Labored breathing, clicking sounds, discharge from eyes or nostrils. Even if only one chick in the bin shows these, skip the whole bin. Mycoplasma and other respiratory pathogens spread fast in a brooder environment.

## Breed Considerations for Feed Store Shoppers

The breeds you'll reliably find at feed stores skew heavily toward commercial layers and dual-purpose birds, which is actually fine for most backyard keepers.

Reliable feed store breeds and what to expect:

- **Rhode Island Reds and Production Reds:** 250-300 eggs per year, good foragers, can be aggressive toward flockmates. Tough birds.
- **Black Sex-Links (also sold as Black Stars):** Calm, excellent layers, easy to sex at hatch. One of the most underrated options in any bin.
- **Buff Orpingtons:** Docile, cold-hardy, 200-230 eggs per year. Go broody more than you'd like if you just want eggs.
- **Barred Plymouth Rocks:** Steady 250+ eggs per year, calm temperament, cold-hardy. Genuinely hard to go wrong here.
- **Easter Eggers (sold as Ameraucanas):** Variable production (150-250 eggs per year), but the colored eggs are worth it for most people.

If the store has Australorps in the bin, buy them. They're consistent layers, calm, and handle both heat and cold better than most breeds get credit for.

## Timing Your Purchase

Don't walk into a feed store in late April expecting a full selection. The best breeds sell out fast, often within hours of a delivery truck. Call the store, find out what day their chicks arrive, and get there that morning. Delivery days vary by store and region, so call ahead to confirm.

**Example:** A store orders 150 chicks weekly from mid-March through late April. Buff Orpingtons and Easter Eggers often sell out by noon on delivery day. If you arrive Thursday, you're picking from whatever's left, which is usually production layers and straight-run birds nobody wanted. Getting there at opening on delivery day gives you first pick of breed and quality.

Special orders solve this problem but require lead time. If you know what you want, ask the store about their hatchery schedule in January or February, and place your order then. You'll typically need to put down a small deposit.

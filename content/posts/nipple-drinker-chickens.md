Most [chicken keepers](/egg-prices-crashed-but-chicken-keepers-are-staying-put/) switch to nipple drinkers and immediately wonder why they waited so long. Then they watch a new pullet stare blankly at the thing for four days and start to second-guess themselves. Both reactions are completely normal.

I've run nipple drinkers in my coops for going on seven years now. Before that, I used the standard gravity bell waterers, the kind where you flip a gallon jug upside down into a plastic base and pretend the algae isn't there. Nipple drinkers fixed almost every problem I had with those, and introduced exactly two new ones (training stubborn birds and winterizing). That tradeoff is worth it every single time.

The core appeal is cleanliness. [Chickens are](/chickens-are-now-americas-third-most-popular-pet/) incapable of not fouling open water. They scratch bedding into it, they step in it, they occasionally try to bathe in it. A horizontal nipple mounted through a bucket or pipe gives them a clean drink without giving them access to the water supply itself. Less litter contamination means less bacteria, fewer respiratory issues, and less time spent scrubbing.

## Nipple Types Actually Worth Buying

There are two main styles: vertical (the pin points down, chicken pecks up at it) and horizontal (the pin points sideways, chicken nudges it from the front or side). I used vertical for my first two years because every forum post at the time recommended them. That was a mistake.

Horizontal nipples are faster to train, drip less, and work better for both bantams and large breeds without adjustment. The vertical style requires the bird to tilt its head back and peck upward, which feels unnatural enough that some birds, especially older hens being converted from open water, just give up. Horizontal nipples mimic the motion of pecking at a water droplet on a leaf. They get it faster.

The brand I keep coming back to is Avian Aqua Miser (around $0.75-$1.25 per nipple, depending on quantity) or the Little Giant horizontal nipples available at most farm supply stores. Current pricing as of August 2026 runs roughly $8-12 for a pack of 10 quality horizontal nipples. Avoid the unmarked bulk packs from overseas sellers at $0.20 each. I bought 50 of those once. Half of them dripped constantly, two cracked during installation, and I ended up replacing them all inside six months.

## Building a Nipple Waterer: The Practical Version

You don't need a kit. A 5-gallon food-grade bucket, a 7/16" drill bit, and four to six horizontal nipples is genuinely all it takes.

Drill your holes in the bottom third of the bucket (not the very bottom, about 2-3 inches up from the base). This placement matters because it keeps pressure consistent and lets you run the bucket nearly dry before water stops flowing. Thread each nipple in by hand until snug, then give it one more quarter-turn with pliers. Don't crank them hard or you'll crack the plastic. A tiny bead of food-safe silicone around the threads is optional but worth it if you've had drip problems.

Hang it so the nipples are at the birds' chest height or slightly higher, not above eye level. This is where I see people go wrong most often. Too high and the birds have to strain upward; too low and they drip water onto themselves and get confused. For standard large-fowl hens, 12-16 inches off the ground usually lands right.

One 5-gallon setup serves 8-12 adult hens without needing daily refills in moderate weather. In July heat, figure on filling every other day.

## Training New and Converted Birds

Chicks under two weeks are actually the easiest to train. Touch the nipple pin gently with your finger until a small drop appears, then guide the chick's beak to it. They'll peck at the movement and get a taste. Done. Most will be self-sufficient within an hour of that first guided contact.

Adult hens switching from open water are the real challenge. My usual protocol: remove the open waterer entirely for 4-6 hours on a mild day (not a 95-degree afternoon), then tap each nipple in front of each hen individually. Some get it immediately. A few stubborn ones will take two days of gentle guidance. I've never had a bird fail to learn it, but I've had a couple try my patience. The key is resisting the urge to put the old waterer back out "just for tonight." That resets the whole process.

Scenario: Three-year-old [Barred Rock](/barred-rock-chickens/) flock of 8 hens, converted in late March 2024. Removed open waterer at 8 a.m., did one training pass with each bird by hand at noon. By the following morning, all eight were using the nipples without prompting. Water consumption was normal by day two.

## Winter: The Part Nobody Warns You About

Nipple drinkers freeze faster than open waterers. The small metal pin and surrounding plastic hold almost no thermal mass, so when temps drop to 32°F overnight, the nipple itself freezes before the water in the bucket does. I learned this the hard way in January 2021 when I walked out to a bucket of liquid water with six completely frozen nipples. The birds were fine; they'd apparently just eaten snow. Still felt terrible.

Two solutions that actually work:

A 50-watt submersible bucket heater (De-Icer brand makes a reliable one, currently around $28-34) keeps the water in the bucket liquid, but won't help the nipples themselves in hard freezes. For the nipples, you need either a heat tape wrap around the bottom of the bucket and nipple area, or a fully enclosed heated waterer designed for nipples.

The better approach for cold climates: build a second open waterer as backup for the three or four months below freezing, and run nipples the other eight months. Trying to nipple-water through a Minnesota winter without serious infrastructure investment isn't worth the headache.

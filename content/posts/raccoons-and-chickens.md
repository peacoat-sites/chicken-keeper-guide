Raccoons kill more [backyard chickens](/are-you-ready-to-raise-backyard-chickens/) than any other predator in North America. Not foxes, not hawks, not neighborhood dogs. Raccoons. The USDA's National Agricultural Statistics Service puts annual poultry losses from predators at roughly 637,000 birds across small and backyard flocks, and wildlife biologists who study urban predation consistently point to *Procyon lotor* as the top culprit in suburban settings. That number doesn't surprise anyone who's walked out to their coop on a Tuesday morning and found the carnage.

Most articles on this topic give you a tidy list of tips and send you on your way. What they skip: raccoons are genuinely intelligent, have hands that operate like a rough draft of human fingers, and they will spend multiple nights testing your coop before they commit to a break-in. They're patient in a way that foxes aren't. I've watched one work a carabiner clip for four minutes on a trail camera before giving up. Four minutes. That kind of persistence changes how you think about latches.

Here's what you actually need to know.

## What Raccoons Actually Do to a Flock

This is where most coverage goes soft. People say "raccoons attacked my chickens" as if it's self-explanatory. Let me be specific, because the type of attack tells you exactly what your coop's vulnerability is.

Reach-through kills are the most common and the most misdiagnosed. A raccoon doesn't need to enter your coop to kill. It reaches through chicken wire or a gap in hardware cloth, grabs a bird by the wing, leg, or head, and pulls until something gives. You'll find birds dead against the wire with missing wings or decapitated. First time I saw this I assumed a weasel. Wrong animal, wrong diagnosis, wrong fix.

Full-entry kills look different: multiple birds dead, some partially eaten (raccoons typically go for the crop and breast), feathers everywhere, and evidence of the coop being opened or forced. If your door latch was defeated, that's your answer.

Raccoons also cache food. They'll kill more than they eat in a single night, which is why you sometimes find three or four dead birds with minimal feeding evidence. It's not spite. It's opportunistic surplus killing, the same behavior documented in mink and foxes. A 2019 study published in *Urban Ecosystems* found raccoon home ranges in suburban areas average 75 to 160 acres, meaning one raccoon can cover your entire neighborhood and rotate through multiple food sources in a week.

## The Hardware Problem Nobody Warns You About

Chicken wire is not predator-proof. I know you've heard this, but let me give you the specifics that make it real.

Standard chicken wire (the hexagonal stuff) has two failures: the wire itself is thin enough that raccoons can tear it with sustained effort, and the openings are large enough for a raccoon's arm. The wire gauges common at farm supply stores run 20-gauge to 22-gauge. A motivated raccoon doesn't need tools; it just needs time.

What actually works is 1/2-inch hardware cloth (welded wire mesh) at 19-gauge or heavier. The [Arnold's Hardware Cloth](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=hardware+cloth+1%2F2+inch+19+gauge&tag=contentportfo-20) sold in 100-foot rolls at around $65 to $90 runs heavier gauge than most big-box alternatives and is worth the extra few dollars per foot. (The site may earn a commission on purchases through these links.)

The second failure is attachment. Raccoons learn fast that staples pull out of wood. A line of poultry staples driven into pine framing will hold for about one season; after that, a raccoon can pop a 6-inch section free with two hands and a little leverage. Use 1/4-inch screws and washers, or fender washers with bolts on any run panels that aren't directly nailed to framing. Takes longer to build. Worth every extra minute.

One detail that matters: overlap your hardware cloth seams by at least 2 inches and fasten through both layers. Seams are where predators start. Every single breach I've seen in a properly-built run started at a seam or an attachment point, not through the middle of a panel.

## Latch Math

Single-pull latches get chickens killed. That's not an opinion; it's a pattern visible across a decade of flock loss reports and my own stupid early mistakes.

Raccoons figured out simple hook-and-eye latches faster than I expected. The first coop I built had a standard barrel bolt on the main pop door. One morning, three months in, I found it open and two hens dead. The bolt had been slid. I don't know how long that took, but it happened.

The solution isn't a padlock (though padlocks work). It's a two-step latch, meaning any latch that requires two distinct sequential actions to open. Raccoons can pull and push and rotate, but coordinating two separate motions in sequence is beyond their problem-solving range in field conditions. The [D&D Technologies TruClose GATE LATCH](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=DD+Technologies+TruClose+gate+latch&tag=contentportfo-20) runs about $18 and requires a lift-and-pull sequence that no raccoon has defeated in my experience or in any credible account I've read. Carabiners clipped through the latch handle add a second layer for under $4 each.

A real-world scenario that illustrates the stakes: a reader named Tammy from outside Columbus emailed me in March 2026 after losing six of her nine Buff Orpingtons in a single night. She had a single barrel-bolt on the coop door and chicken wire on the run. Total bird replacement cost at $30 to $45 per laying Buff: roughly $270 in stock, plus emotional loss, plus the time rebuilding her setup. Rebuilding with hardware cloth and two-step latches ran her about $140 in materials. She's had zero losses in the five months since.

## Predator-Proofing by Component

Not every part of your coop carries equal risk. Here's how I rank the vulnerabilities and what each fix costs, as of August 2026:

| Component | Vulnerability | Fix | Approximate Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pop door latch | Single-pull defeat | Two-step latch + carabiner | $22 to $28 |
| Run wire (chicken wire) | Reach-through, tear-through | Replace with 1/2" 19-ga hardware cloth | $65 to $120 (per 100 ft) |
| Wire attachment | Staple pullout | Fender washer + screw system | $12 to $20 per run panel |
| Run floor (no apron) | Digging entry | Buried apron (12" out, 6" down) | $40 to $80 in labor + material |
| Roof/top of run | Climbing entry | Hardware cloth roof or electric wire at 8" height | $30 to $90 |
| Windows/vents | Reach-through | Cover all openings with hardware cloth | $15 to $40 |
| Main coop door | Human-style latch defeat | Padlock or two-step latch | $15 to $35 |

The buried apron is the one people skip because it involves digging. Don't skip it. Raccoons dig, and a standard skirt laid on top of the ground gets moved. Go down 6 inches at the wall, then angle the bottom outward 12 inches horizontally. When a raccoon digs at the base, it hits wire immediately and gives up. This design is backed by recommendations from the University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources extension program, which has published predator exclusion guides specifically for small-scale poultry.

## Electric Wire: Overkill or Obvious

I resisted electric fencing for years. Seemed like overkill for a suburban backyard. I was wrong, and the thing that changed my mind was a trail camera.

After a run rebuild that I thought was solid, the camera caught a raccoon spending 22 minutes on three separate nights testing every seam and latch on my run. It never got in, but watching that footage made me realize I was in a slow-burn arms race and I was going to lose eventually. I added a single strand of electric fence wire (Premier 1's [IntelliShock 30 energizer](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Premier+1+IntelliShock+30+fence+energizer&tag=contentportfo-20), around $75) at 8 inches off the ground around the run perimeter. The raccoon appeared on camera twice more in the following two weeks, touched the wire once, and hasn't returned in over a year.

One strand. Eight inches. That's it.

Premier 1's energizer is slightly overbuilt for a typical backyard run, but it means you'll never wonder if the charge is adequate. For smaller setups, the Zareba AC-powered energizers run $45 to $55 and cover up to half a mile of fence, plenty for 200 feet of perimeter.

Worth noting: [electric fence](/electric-fence-for-chickens/) isn't a standalone solution. If a raccoon somehow bypasses or ignores the wire (this is rare but happens with very bold animals), your mechanical barriers still need to hold. Electric is a first-line deterrent, not a replacement for hardware cloth and proper latches.

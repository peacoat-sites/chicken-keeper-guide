Most [backyard flock](/h5n1-can-kill-your-cat-what-backyard-flock-owners-must-know/) keepers hit the same wall around year three: the hens are healthy, the coop is clean, and the egg production has quietly fallen off a cliff. What do you actually do with a retired laying hen? You have three real options: keep her as a pet, rehome her, or process her for meat. None of them is universally right, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

The honest calculus depends on your setup, your emotional relationship with the bird, and whether you can stomach the feed cost of a hen laying two or three eggs a week instead of five or six. A heavy dual-purpose breed like a Barred [Plymouth Rock](/plymouth-rock-chickens/) or a Buff Orpington will slow down but not stop completely. A production-focused Leghorn, though, often goes from machine to near-zero in a matter of months.

## The Feed Math You Should Actually Do

Before you decide anything, run the numbers. A hen eating a quarter pound of feed per day at current layer pellet prices (roughly $20 to $30 per 50-lb bag in most U.S. markets) costs somewhere between $55 and $85 [per year](/egg-production-per-year-by-breed/) just in feed, before bedding, healthcare, or coop overhead.

**Example:** Assume a 50-lb bag costs $25 and lasts one hen about 200 days (at 0.25 lb/day). That's about $45.63 per year in feed alone per bird. Add $10 to $15 in bedding and you're at roughly $55 to $60 per hen annually. A retired hen laying 50 eggs per year at a generous retail value of $0.50 per egg returns $25 in production value. Net cost: $30 to $35 per bird per year, minimum, with no labor factored in.

That's a reasonable pet expense for a bird you care about. It's worth spelling out so you're not surprised.

## Keeping Her in the Flock

Retired hens can serve a real function even without consistent laying. Older hens often establish and maintain flock order, which reduces bullying among younger birds. If you're doing rolling additions to your flock every few years, a calm older hen can smooth the integration process considerably.

The practical concern is whether a retired hen is being picked on rather than respected. Watch for feather loss around the neck and back, reduced access to feeders, or the hen sleeping apart from the group. Any of those signs means she's at the bottom of the order, and a bottom-of-the-order hen who isn't laying has a harder time justifying her spot from a flock-health standpoint.

There's no rule that says you have to process a hen who isn't laying enough. Plenty of people keep hens until natural death. That's a personal call, not a failure of chicken keeping.

## Processing for Meat: What to Expect

This is where a lot of guides get soft. A two- or three-year-old laying hen is not a broiler. She will be tough if you roast her like one. The meat is darker, more flavorful, and genuinely good, but it needs moisture and time.

| Method | Minimum Cook Time | Best Results |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure cooker | 45-60 minutes | Pulled chicken, soups |
| Slow cooker (low) | 6-8 hours | Stock, stews, braises |
| Dutch oven braise | 2.5-3.5 hours at 300°F | Coq au vin, fricassee |
| Roasting (dry heat) | Not recommended | Meat will be very tough |

An older hen makes exceptional stock. The higher collagen content from years of movement produces a richer, more gelatinous broth than you'll get from a young broiler.

If you're processing at home for the first time, the Humane Slaughter Association publishes guidance on small-flock poultry slaughter that covers the mechanics clearly and without sensationalism. If home processing isn't feasible, look for a USDA-inspected poultry processor in your area, though many won't take small numbers of birds. Farm-to-table Facebook groups and local feed store bulletin boards are often the fastest way to find someone nearby who processes small batches.

## Rehoming: Be Honest With Yourself

Retired laying hens are not easy to rehome. Local Facebook poultry groups are flooded with free hens every spring and fall, and demand is low unless someone specifically wants pet chickens or is comfortable processing them. Rescue organizations that accept chickens exist, but they're not common and they're often at capacity.

If you do find a taker, be upfront about the bird's age and production level. Don't advertise a 3-year-old Leghorn as a "laying hen" to someone expecting a full season of eggs. That's just passing your problem on and burning a relationship.

Breed matters here. A large, friendly dual-purpose hen like an Orpington or a Wyandotte has a better chance of finding a pet home than a flighty production-breed white egg layer. Know what you have before you post.

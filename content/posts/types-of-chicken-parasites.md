Parasites are one of the most underdiscussed threats to backyard flocks, and they're common enough that if you keep chickens long enough, you will deal with them. The good news: most are manageable once you know what you're looking for. The bad news: a surprising number of keepers don't notice an infestation until it's serious, because they confuse a dull molt or slow laying with something unrelated.

There are two broad categories. External parasites live on the skin, feathers, and scales. Internal parasites live inside the bird. Both can tank egg production, suppress immunity, cause anemia, and kill birds, particularly young chicks or hens already stressed by heat, poor nutrition, or overcrowding. The mistake most beginners make is assuming their birds are fine because they look fine. By the time a chicken looks sick from a parasite load, it's often been struggling for weeks.

## External Parasites: The Ones You Can See (If You Look)

**Mites** are the big one. There are several species, and they behave differently enough that lumping them together leads to misdiagnosis.

Red mites (*Dermanyssus gallinae*) are arguably the most dangerous because of how they hide. During daylight hours they tuck into cracks in roosts, under nesting box pads, and in any gap in the coop structure. They only come out at night to feed. So your birds can look fine during a midday inspection while hosting an enormous population that's slowly bleeding them every night. Check your coop after dark with a flashlight, run your finger along roost undersides, and look for dusty reddish-brown streaks that smear red when crushed. That's them. A heavy red mite load in a small coop can kill adult hens through anemia and will kill chicks fast.

Northern fowl mites (*Ornithonyssus sylviarum*) are easier to spot because they stay on the bird. Look at the vent area and under the wings. You'll see grayish debris and sometimes visible movement. The base of feathers near the vent often gets darkly discolored from mite feces and egg cases. These mites are most common in fall and winter.

Scaly leg mites (*Knemidocoptes mutans*) burrow under the leg scales and cause legs to look rough, crusty, and lifted. It's a slow infestation. Treatment involves smothering: petroleum jelly applied thoroughly under the scales several times a week for weeks. Some keepers use Vaseline, some use coconut oil, some use a diluted permethrin dip. All work if applied consistently. The scales won't look normal immediately after treatment; it takes a full molt cycle for them to grow back cleanly.

**Lice** are another common culprit. They're fast-moving, pale yellowish insects that live their entire life cycle on the bird, which is different from most mite species. Chicken lice eat skin debris, not blood, so they're slightly less immediately dangerous than mites, but they cause intense irritation and birds will scratch and preen excessively, lose condition, and lay inconsistently. Check the vent area and under the wings. Louse eggs cluster at the base of feathers in white or pale clumps. If you see those clumps, you have a confirmed infestation.

**Fleas** can infest coops, particularly in warmer climates or in coops built on dirt. The sticktight flea (*Echidnophaga gallinacea*) is especially problematic in the South and Southwest; it embeds itself into the skin around the head and face. Treatment requires removing embedded fleas with tweezers and treating the coop environment aggressively.

Here's a comparison of the most common external parasites so you can tell them apart at a glance:

| Parasite | Location on Bird | Visible During Day? | Main Symptom | Treatment Approach |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Red mite | Off bird (hides in coop) | No, check at night | Anemia, pale comb, restlessness | Treat coop: permethrin spray, diatomaceous earth, clean all cracks |
| Northern fowl mite | On bird (vent, under wings) | Yes | Vent feather darkening, itching | Permethrin or pyrethrin dust on bird and in coop |
| Scaly leg mite | Under leg scales | Yes (slow to notice) | Lifted, crusty scales | Petroleum jelly or ivermectin topically, repeated weekly |
| Poultry lice | On bird (vent, wings) | Yes | Feather damage, restlessness, pale clumps at feather base | Permethrin or pyrethrin dust on bird |
| Sticktight flea | Embedded in face/head skin | Yes | Crusted lesions on face | Manual removal + environmental insecticide |

## Internal Parasites: Worms and Protozoa

Most backyard flocks kept on pasture or dirt carry some internal parasite load. The question is whether that load is high enough to cause disease.

**Roundworms** (*Ascaridia galli*) are the most common intestinal worm in chickens. A light infection may show no symptoms. A heavy one causes weight loss, diarrhea, and can actually cause intestinal blockage in severe cases. Eggs are shed in droppings and can survive in soil for months, which is why pasture rotation matters.

**Cecal worms** (*Heterakis gallinarum*) live in the ceca and are significant partly because they can carry *Histomonas meleagridis*, the protozoan responsible for blackhead disease. Blackhead is devastating in turkeys but can affect chickens too. If you keep both species together, cecal worms become a serious risk.

**Capillary worms** (capillaria, also called hairworms) are thin, threadlike worms that can infect the crop, esophagus, and intestinal tract. They cause weight loss and poor condition and are harder to treat because some species have an earthworm intermediate host. If your birds have access to earthworms and you're seeing persistent poor condition despite good nutrition, capillaria is worth considering.

**Tapeworms** are less common than roundworms but do occur. They require intermediate hosts, usually beetles, flies, slugs, or earthworms, so birds with extensive access to pasture are more exposed. Tapeworm infections are notoriously hard to confirm without a fecal float test, and treatment options in backyard chickens are limited.

**Coccidia** are protozoa, not worms, but they belong in any discussion of internal poultry parasites. *Eimeria* species cause coccidiosis, which is especially dangerous in chicks. Bloody diarrhea in birds under 12 weeks is a coccidiosis emergency. Amprolium (Corid) is the standard treatment: mix according to label directions and provide as the only water source for 5 to 7 days. Adult birds often carry coccidia without clinical disease; chicks raised on medicated chick starter develop gradual immunity.

## Diagnosing What You're Actually Dealing With

Don't guess if the bird isn't improving. A fecal float test run by a vet or through a mail-in lab service (several exist in the US, including Veterinary Parasitology Laboratory at Texas A&M) can confirm worm species and provide egg counts. That changes your treatment decisions, especially for tapeworms, which don't respond to fenbendazole.

For external parasites, the physical inspection is your diagnostic tool. Use a flashlight. Part the feathers. Check the vent area. Inspect the coop at night. These steps will tell you more than any product label.

## Treatment Options and What to Know Before You Use Them

Permethrin-based dusts and sprays are the standard for external parasites. Pyrethrin products work similarly. Apply to birds directly (avoid the face and eyes) and treat the coop environment simultaneously, because treating only the bird while the coop is still infested just reinfects them.

Ivermectin is used by many backyard keepers for both external and some internal parasites, usually applied topically at the back of the neck. The key fact: ivermectin is not approved by the FDA for use in laying hens in the United States, meaning there's no established egg withdrawal period. Some sources suggest 7 to 14 days, but that's not an official figure. Know this before treating laying hens.

Fenbendazole (sold as SafeGuard Goat Dewormer or Panacur) is the most accessible broad-spectrum wormer for backyard keepers. It treats roundworms, capillaria, and some other species. It does not treat tapeworms. Again, no FDA-approved label for laying hens, so egg withdrawal is a gray area. Many keepers follow a conservative 14-day withdrawal.

**Example:** A keeper with 8 adult hens suspects a worm burden after noticing weight loss in two birds and finding loose, pale droppings. Off-label dosing for fenbendazole in chickens varies widely in published guidance and must be confirmed with a veterinarian before use. SafeGuard Goat Dewormer is 100 mg/mL, so that's a small volume. Exact dosing should be confirmed with a veterinarian because off-label use means you're responsible for the calculation. A fecal float before and after confirms whether the protocol worked.

One note on diatomaceous earth: food-grade DE is widely promoted for mites and internal parasites. The evidence for it as an internal wormer is weak. As a dry dust in the coop and in dust-bath areas, it may help with external parasites to some degree. Don't rely on it as your only control strategy, and be careful about inhalation risk for both birds and humans.

## Prevention That Actually Works

Overcrowding is the single biggest risk factor for rapid parasite buildup. A stressed, crowded flock reinfects itself faster than you can treat it. If your birds are sharing a small coop and run with no access to fresh ground, you're fighting an uphill battle. Use our [Coop Size Calc](/chicken-coop-size-calculator/) to check whether your setup gives birds adequate space before the problem starts.

Rotate pasture access when possible. Rest a run for 60 to 90 days to break the soil cycle for many worm species. Add a dry dust bath area filled with fine dirt and wood ash year-round; birds naturally dust-bathe to control external parasites, and removing that option leaves them without a primary defense.

Monthly inspection is non-negotiable. Five minutes of checking each bird's vent area and legs, plus a quick coop inspection, will catch most infestations before they become crises.

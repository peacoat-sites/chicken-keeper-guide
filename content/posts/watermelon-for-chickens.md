Watermelon is one of the safest, most practical summer treats you can give laying hens. Yes to the flesh, yes to the rind, yes to the seeds. No prep required beyond halving it and setting it on the ground.

That said, "safe" and "unlimited" are not the same thing. Treats of any kind, watermelon included, dilute the nutritional profile of the diet if they crowd out complete feed. The 90/10 rule is a reasonable target: 90% of daily intake from a balanced layer feed, 10% from treats. For a standard six-hen [backyard flock](/what-the-cdcs-new-backyard-flock-survey-found-about-hpai/), that's not a lot of watermelon before you've hit the ceiling.

## What's Actually in Watermelon

Watermelon runs about 92% water by weight, with modest natural sugars and small amounts of vitamin C, vitamin A (from lycopene), and potassium. It's not a nutritional powerhouse for chickens the way black soldier fly larvae or mealworms are. What it does well is hydration. On days above 85-90°F, hens drink less than they should, egg quality can drop, and heat stress sets in faster than most people expect. Getting extra fluid into them through feed is a proven, low-effort strategy.

The seeds are not a problem. Watermelon seeds are soft enough that chickens handle them without issue, and there's no compound in them that's harmful to poultry. The rind is fine too, though the white-green inner rind is more palatable to them than the tough outer skin. Most flocks will peck the rind down significantly but leave the waxy outer layer.

## How Much, How Often

Here's a simple working example.

**Example:** A flock of eight hens eating 0.25 lbs of layer feed each per day has a total daily feed intake of 2 lbs. Ten percent of that is 3.2 oz, or roughly the volume of a 1-inch-thick quarter slice of a standard watermelon. That's the daily treat ceiling for the whole flock, not per bird.

That's not very much. A lot of [backyard keepers](/new-usda-poultry-rules-are-now-in-effect-here-is-what-backyard-keepers/) set out a whole half-watermelon and let birds free-feed on it. That's fine as a once-in-a-while thing, particularly on a very hot day when you want them hydrated. But if you're doing it daily through a long summer, you'll likely see some loose droppings (normal with high-water-content foods, not a health concern at moderate amounts) and potentially a slight softening of shells if the birds are filling up on watermelon instead of their layer ration.

A rough frequency guide, assuming a balanced [layer feed](/layer-feed-vs-starter-feed/) is always available:

| Flock size | Reasonable portion per session | Suggested max frequency |
|------------|-------------------------------|------------------------|
| 2-4 hens | 1/4 of a small melon | Every other day in summer |
| 5-8 hens | 1/2 of a small melon | Every other day in summer |
| 9-15 hens | 1 whole small melon | Daily in peak heat (90°F+) |
| 16+ hens | 1-2 melons | Daily in peak heat, monitor feed intake |

These aren't hard limits. They're starting points. If you're seeing wet droppings consistently, pulled back a little. If egg production dips and nothing else has changed, check whether the treat volume has crept up.

## The Frozen Trick

Slice watermelon into 1-2 inch chunks, spread them on a baking sheet, freeze until solid, then transfer to a bag. On days when the temperature is forecast above 95°F, scatter frozen chunks in a shaded area of the run mid-morning. Birds peck at them for 20-30 minutes, get hydration, and the cold is a mild temperature buffer. It's also one of the few hot-weather interventions that takes under two minutes to execute once you've got a bag ready in the freezer.

This matters because heat stress in laying hens isn't just an animal welfare issue. Hens under sustained heat stress lay fewer eggs, lay thinner-shelled eggs, and are more susceptible to respiratory illness.

## What to Skip

Don't offer watermelon that's been sitting out in heat for several hours. Bacteria multiply fast in the high-sugar, high-moisture environment of cut melon, and chickens are not immune to bacterial digestive upsets. Set it out in the morning, let them work on it for an hour or two, then remove it.

Moldy rind is a harder no. Mold on food can produce mycotoxins, and while chickens are more resistant to some mycotoxins than other livestock, it's not a risk worth taking when tossing a rind in the compost is the alternative.

Avoid any watermelon that's been seasoned, salted, or otherwise prepared for human consumption. That should be obvious, but it comes up.
